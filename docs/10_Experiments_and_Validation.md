# 10. Experiments and Validation

To mathematically prove the correctness of the **Gradience** framework—from its core autograd engine to its high-level neural network modules—we conducted a series of end-to-end integration experiments. 

In these experiments, we implement standard machine learning tasks natively in Gradience and compare the training trajectories (loss curves and accuracies) directly against identical models built using the industry-standard **PyTorch** framework. 

All experimental notebooks can be found in the `examples/` directory.

---

## Experiment 1: Linear Regression
**File:** `examples/linear_regression.ipynb`

### Objective
To verify that the fundamental autograd engine calculates gradients correctly for basic arithmetic operations and that the SGD optimizer properly traverses a convex error surface.

### Architecture
*   **Model**: A single `Linear(1, 1)` layer mapping 1 input feature to 1 output feature.
*   **Loss Function**: `MSELoss`.
*   **Optimizer**: `SGD` with a learning rate of $0.1$.
*   **Dataset**: Synthetic data generated via the function $y = 2.0x + 1.0$ plus Gaussian noise.

### Results
Both Gradience and PyTorch perfectly recovered the underlying weight ($\approx 2.0$) and bias ($\approx 1.0$). When plotting the loss curves over 100 epochs, the Gradience loss trajectory mapped exactly 1:1 on top of the PyTorch loss trajectory, proving mathematical equivalence for single-layer optimizations.

---

## Experiment 2: Non-Linear XOR Classification
**File:** `examples/train_xor.ipynb`

### Objective
To verify that Gradience can correctly backpropagate through non-linear activation functions (`Tanh`, `Sigmoid`) across multiple layers in a computational graph. XOR is famously not linearly separable, making it the perfect benchmark for non-linear feature projection.

### Architecture
*   **Model**: A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) built with `Sequential`.
    *   `Linear(2, 8)`
    *   `Tanh()`
    *   `Linear(8, 1)`
    *   `Sigmoid()`
*   **Loss Function**: `MSELoss`.
*   **Optimizer**: `SGD` with a learning rate of $0.5$.
*   **Dataset**: The standard XOR truth table.

### Results
Gradience successfully drove the MSE loss to effectively $0.0$ over 5,000 epochs, successfully pulling apart the non-linear space. When comparing the probability contour map generated by the final Sigmoid layer, Gradience and PyTorch converged on the exact same decision boundaries and output identical loss curves.

---

## Experiment 3: MNIST Handwritten Digit Recognition
**File:** `examples/train_mnist.ipynb`

### Objective
To stress-test the framework on a high-dimensional, real-world dataset. This verifies the stability of the autograd engine during complex batched matrix multiplications, tracking gradients across thousands of data points and large weight tensors.

### Architecture
*   **Model**: A deep MLP designed to classify 10 separate digits.
    *   `Linear(784, 128)`
    *   `ReLU()`
    *   `Linear(128, 10)`
    *   `Sigmoid()` (Approximating multi-label classification since Softmax is under development).
*   **Loss Function**: `MSELoss` against one-hot encoded targets.
*   **Optimizer**: `SGD` with a learning rate of $1.0$ (adjusted for batch averaging).
*   **Training Loop**: Mini-batch processing over a subset of 10,000 MNIST training samples using a batch size of $64$, running for 20 epochs. Validation is checked post-epoch.

### Results
Even on a large dataset with thousands of parameters, the computational graph constructed dynamically by Gradience held up flawlessly without memory leaks or graph tearing. The training loss rapidly decreased, and validation accuracy steadily increased, achieving identical classification accuracy when compared step-for-step with PyTorch. 

---

## Conclusion
These three experiments definitively prove that **Gradience** is a fully functional, mathematically precise deep learning framework. The native implementations of `Sequential`, `Linear`, `ReLU`, `Sigmoid`, `Tanh`, and `SGD` successfully work together to drive gradient descent across both simple and highly non-convex surfaces exactly as PyTorch does.
